    Worksheet Apportionment as Geometry: Hamilton and Webster on the Simplex
    

    In this activity you will treat apportionment methods as functions from a continuous quota space to discrete integer allocations. You will visualize Hamilton’s method and Webster’s method as geometric partitions of a simplex.
    🔗
    Throughout this worksheet we work on the simplex \(\Delta_4=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^3 : x+y+z=4\}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Warm-Up: Two Groups, Five Seats.

    Before working in triangles and three dimensions, we begin with the simplest possible case: two groups sharing a fixed number of seats.
    🔗
    Suppose two groups, A and B, are dividing 5 seats. If group A receives \(a\) seats, then group B receives \(5-a\text{.}\) So every possible allocation corresponds to a point on the line segment
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0 \le a \le 5. \end{equation*}
    In this activity, you will compare Hamilton’s method and Webster’s method in this one-dimensional setting.
    🔗
    🔗

    Quota Space on a Line.

    Think of \(a\) as the (possibly fractional) quota for group A. The quota for group B is automatically \(5-a\text{.}\)
    🔗

    1.

    Mark the integer points on the number line from 0 to 5.
    🔗
    What do these integer points represent in terms of seat allocations?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The integer points are \(0,1,2,3,4,5\text{.}\) The point \(k\) represents the allocation “A gets \(k\) seats and B gets \(5-k\) seats,” i.e. \((k,5-k)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    What does an integer like \(a=2.7\) (or the ordered pair \((2.7,2.3)\)) represent in this setting?
    🔗
    Solution.
    It represents a quota (ideal fractional allocation) where A “deserves” about \(2.7\) seats and B about \(5-2.7=2.3\) seats. An apportionment method will turn this into an integer allocation near \((3,2)\) or \((2,3)\text{.}\) Which one depends on the method.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Hamilton’s Method on the Line.

    Hamilton’s method works by first taking lower quotas and then assigning leftover seats based on the size of the fractional parts.
    🔗
    In the two-group case, this process simplifies dramatically!
    🔗

    3.

    Suppose group A has quota \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Write the lower quota for group A.
    🔗
    (b) Write the lower quota for group B.
    🔗
    (c) How many seats remain to be assigned after taking floors?
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) A’s lower quota is \(\lfloor a\rfloor\text{.}\) (b) B’s lower quota is \(\lfloor 5-a\rfloor\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) The remaining seats are \(5-(\lfloor a\rfloor+\lfloor 5-a\rfloor)\text{.}\) For non-integer \(a\text{,}\) we have \(\lfloor a\rfloor+\lfloor 5-a\rfloor=4\text{,}\) so there is 1 leftover seat. (If \(a\) is an integer, there are no leftover seats.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Show that Hamilton’s method gives the extra seat to group A exactly when
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} a - \lfloor a \rfloor \ge (5-a) - \lfloor 5-a \rfloor. \end{equation*}
    Simplify this inequality.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Write the remainders: \(r_A=a-\lfloor a\rfloor\) and \(r_B=(5-a)-\lfloor 5-a\rfloor\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For non-integer \(a\text{,}\) \(\lceil a\rceil=\lfloor a\rfloor+1\) and \(\lfloor 5-a\rfloor=5-\lceil a\rceil\text{,}\) so \(r_B=(5-a)-(5-\lceil a\rceil)=\lceil a\rceil-a=\lfloor a\rfloor+1-a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then \(r_A \ge r_B\) becomes
    \begin{equation*} a-\lfloor a\rfloor \ge \lfloor a\rfloor+1-a \auad\Longleftrightarrow\auad 2a \ge 2\lfloor a\rfloor+1 \auad\Longleftrightarrow\auad a \ge \lfloor a\rfloor+\tfrac12. \end{equation*}
    So, within each unit interval \([k,k+1)\text{,}\) the “switch point” is \(k+\tfrac12\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Find the value of \(a\) where the seat allocation switches.
    🔗
    Draw this point on the number line and label the intervals where Hamilton assigns:
    🔗
    • 2 seats to A,
      🔗
    • 3 seats to A.
      🔗
    Solution.
    The switch between A getting 2 vs 3 seats occurs on the interval \([2,3)\text{,}\) at \(a=2.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(2 \le a \lt 2.5\text{,}\) Hamilton gives A 2 seats (and B 3). For \(2.5 \le a \lt 3\text{,}\) Hamilton gives A 3 seats (and B 2). (At exactly \(a=2.5\) there is a tie in remainders.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Webster’s Method on the Line.

    Webster’s method uses a scaling factor (the divisor) and then rounds to the nearest integer.
    🔗
    In the two-group case, Webster’s behavior becomes especially simple.
    🔗

    6.

    Suppose Webster uses the quota \(a\) directly. Write the condition under which group A is rounded to 3 seats.
    🔗
    Express your answer as an inequality involving \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

    Find the value of \(a\) where Webster switches from giving A 2 seats to giving A 3 seats.
    🔗
    Mark this point on the same number line you used for Hamilton.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Huntington–Hill (Equal Proportions) on the Line.

    Now we add one more method, often called Huntington–Hill or Equal Proportions. In this warm-up we will describe it as a next-seat rule.
    🔗
    We will use the common convention that each group must receive at least 1 seat. So with 5 total seats and two groups, the possible final allocations are \((1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 1: A next-seat score.

    Suppose a group has population \(p\) and currently has \(n\) seats. Its score for receiving the next seat is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{score}(p,n)=\frac{p}{\sqrt{n(n+1)}}. \end{equation*}
    The next seat goes to the group with the larger score. (If there is a tie, use a tie-breaking rule.)
    🔗

    8.

    Starting from \((1,1)\text{,}\) there are 3 more seats to give out. Make a small table of the scores that would be compared at each step:
    🔗
    • When comparing A goes from 1 to 2 versus B goes from 1 to 2.
      🔗
    • When comparing A goes from 2 to 3 versus B goes from 1 to 2.
      🔗
    • When comparing A goes from 3 to 4 versus B goes from 1 to 2.
      🔗
    Solution.
    The score for going from \(n\) to \(n+1\) is \(p/\sqrt{n(n+1)}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Comparing A: \(1 \to 2\) versus B: \(1 \to 2\) compares \(\dfrac{p_A}{\sqrt{1\cdot 2}}\) to \(\dfrac{p_B}{\sqrt{1\cdot 2}}\text{,}\) so the first extra seat goes to whichever population is larger.
    🔗
    Later comparisons involve different denominators: A: \(2\to 3\) uses \(\sqrt{2\cdot 3}=\sqrt{6}\text{,}\) while B: \(1\to 2\) uses \(\sqrt{2}\text{,}\) etc.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 2: Where does the outcome switch?

    As before, let \(a\) be A’s quota on the line, so B’s quota is \(5-a\text{.}\) The Huntington–Hill method partitions the line into intervals where A receives 1, 2, 3, or 4 seats.
    🔗

    9.

    The middle switch between A getting 2 seats versus 3 seats happens at \(a=2.5\text{.}\) (This is the symmetric point where A and B have equal population.)
    🔗
    The other two switch points occur when the population ratio reaches \(\sqrt{6}\) (or its reciprocal). Use the score comparison to show that the boundary between outcomes \((1,4)\) and \((2,3)\) occurs when
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{p_A}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{p_B}{\sqrt{6}}. \end{equation*}
    Then solve for the corresponding quota value \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The boundary between final outcomes \((1,4)\) and \((2,3)\) is the point where, after A already has 1 seat, A is tied with B for receiving the next relevant seat. One clean way to capture the decisive tie is: compare A’s score for \(1\to 2\) with B’s score for \(2\to 3\) (which uses \(\sqrt{6}\)). Setting them equal gives \(\dfrac{p_A}{\sqrt{2}}=\dfrac{p_B}{\sqrt{6}}\text{,}\) i.e. \(\dfrac{p_A}{p_B}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{6}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\text{.}\) (Equivalently, a symmetric formulation yields the same quota cut point.)
    🔗
    A more standard (and symmetric) way to express the decisive cut points for 5 seats is: A receives 1 seat when \(\dfrac{p_A}{p_B} \lt \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\text{,}\) and A receives 4 seats when \(\dfrac{p_A}{p_B} \gt \sqrt{6}\text{.}\) Converting to quota on the line: \(a=5\cdot \dfrac{p_A}{p_A+p_B}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(\dfrac{p_A}{p_B}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\text{,}\) then \(\dfrac{p_A}{p_A+p_B}=\dfrac{1/\sqrt{6}}{1+1/\sqrt{6}}=\dfrac{1}{1+\sqrt{6}}\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} a=\frac{5}{1+\sqrt{6}}\approx 1.4494897. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    By symmetry, the boundary between 3 and 4 seats occurs at
    \begin{equation*} a=\frac{5\sqrt{6}}{1+\sqrt{6}}\approx 3.5505103. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    10.

    Use the switch points to label the intervals on the line where A receives 1, 2, 3, or 4 seats under Huntington–Hill.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(t_a=\dfrac{5}{1+\sqrt{6}}\approx 1.4494897\) and \(t_b=\dfrac{5\sqrt{6}}{1+\sqrt{6}}\approx 3.5505103\text{.}\)
    🔗
    • \(0 \le a \lt t_a\text{:}\) A gets 1 seat (allocation \((1,4)\)).
      🔗
    • \(a \le a \lt 2.5\text{:}\) A gets 2 seats (allocation \((2,3)\)).
      🔗
    • \(2.5 \le a \lt t_b\text{:}\) A gets 3 seats (allocation \((3,2)\)).
      🔗
    • \(b \le a \le 5\text{:}\) A gets 4 seats (allocation \((4,1)\)).
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Dean’s Method on the Line.

    Dean’s method is another divisor method, similar in structure to Webster’s and Huntington–Hill’s. The difference lies in how rounding is decided.
    🔗
    Instead of rounding to the nearest integer or using geometric means, Dean’s method uses the harmonic mean as the cutoff between adjacent integers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 1: Dean’s Rounding Rule.

    Suppose a quota value \(q\) lies between two integers \(n\) and \(n+1\text{.}\) Dean’s method rounds:
    🔗
    • down to \(n\) if \(q\) is less than the harmonic mean of \(n\) and \(n+1\text{,}\)
      🔗
    • up to \(n+1\) if \(q\) is greater than that harmonic mean.
      🔗
    Recall that the harmonic mean of \(n\) and \(n+1\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H(n,n+1)=\frac{2n(n+1)}{2n+1}. \end{equation*}

    11.

    Compute the harmonic mean cutoff between:
    🔗
    • 1 and 2
      🔗
    • 2 and 3
      🔗
    Solution.
    Between 1 and 2:
    \begin{equation*} H(1,2)=\frac{2\cdot1\cdot2}{2\cdot1+1}=\frac{4}{3}\approx1.3333. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Between 2 and 3:
    \begin{equation*} H(2,3)=\frac{2\cdot2\cdot3}{2\cdot2+1}=\frac{12}{5}=2.4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 2: Dean’s Switching Points on the Line.

    As before, let \(a\) represent A’s quota on the line with total 5 seats. Dean’s rounding thresholds determine when A switches from receiving 2 seats to 3 seats, and from 1 to 2, etc.
    🔗

    12.

    Using the harmonic mean cutoff between 2 and 3, determine the value of \(a\) where Dean switches from assigning A 2 seats to assigning A 3 seats.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The cutoff between 2 and 3 is \(2.4\text{.}\) So Dean rounds to 3 seats when \(a \ge 2.4\text{,}\) and to 2 seats when \(a \lt 2.4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    13.

    Use the harmonic mean cutoffs to find the approximate switch points where A moves:
    🔗
    • from 1 seat to 2 seats,
      🔗
    • from 3 seats to 4 seats.
      🔗
    Solution.
    Between 1 and 2 seats, the cutoff is \(4/3 \approx 1.333\text{.}\) So A switches from 1 to 2 seats at \(a \approx 1.333\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By symmetry, the cutoff between 3 and 4 seats occurs at \(5-4/3=3.6667\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    14.

    Label the intervals on the number line where Dean assigns A:
    🔗
    • 1 seat
      🔗
    • 2 seats
      🔗
    • 3 seats
      🔗
    • 4 seats
      🔗
    Solution.
    Approximately:
    🔗
    • \(0 \le a \lt 1.333\text{:}\) A gets 1 seat
      🔗
    • \(1.333 \le a \lt 2.4\text{:}\) A gets 2 seats
      🔗
    • \(2.4 \le a \lt 3.667\text{:}\) A gets 3 seats
      🔗
    • \(3.667 \le a \le 5\text{:}\) A gets 4 seats
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Comparing All Four Methods.

    15.

    Compare the switching points for Hamilton, Webster, Huntington–Hill, and Dean for the transition between 2 seats and 3 seats.
    🔗
    Arrange the methods from earliest switch to latest switch as \(a\) increases.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Switching points for A going from 2 to 3 seats:
    🔗
    • Dean: \(a=2.4\)
      🔗
    • Hamilton: \(a=2.5\)
      🔗
    • Webster: \(a=2.5\)
      🔗
    • Huntington–Hill: \(a=2.5\)
      🔗
    So Dean switches earliest, while the other three coincide at the midpoint for this case.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    16.

    In words: what does Dean’s method favor compared to Webster and Huntington–Hill?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Dean’s method favors giving additional seats slightly earlier (at smaller quota values). Geometrically, its rounding boundaries are shifted toward the lower integer compared to arithmetic and geometric mean rounding.

    Barycentric Coordinates and the Simplex.

    The plane slice \(x+y+z=4\) in the positive orthant is represented by an equilateral triangle. Each point in the triangle corresponds to a triple with nonnegative coordinates summing to 4. The vertices correspond to \((4,0,0)\text{,}\) \((0,4,0)\text{,}\) and \((0,0,4)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Hamilton’s Method as a Geometric Function.

    Hamilton’s method assigns seats by first taking lower quotas and then distributing the remaining seats according to the sizes of the fractional parts (remainders). In this section, you will build Hamilton regions step by step.
    🔗
    Throughout, we work on the simplex \(\Delta_4=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^3 : x+y+z=4\}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 20. Hamilton’s Method.

    1. Compute the lower quota vector \(\ell=(\lfloor x\rfloor,\lfloor y\rfloor,\lfloor z\rfloor)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Let \(s=4-(\ell_1+\ell_2+\ell_3)\) be the number of surplus seats.
      🔗
    3. Let \(r_i=q_i-\lfloor q_i\rfloor\) be the remainders. Let \(S(q)\) be the set of indices corresponding to the \(s\) largest remainders.
      🔗
    4. Define \(H(q)=\ell+\sum_{i\in S(q)} e_i\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗

    Step 1: Floor Slabs.

    The first step of Hamilton’s method depends only on the floor function. This divides the simplex into rectangular “slabs” where the lower quota vector \(\ell\) is constant.
    🔗

    🔗
    (b) Compute the number of surplus seats \(s\text{.}\)

    🔗

    These comparisons determine which coordinates receive the surplus seats.

    Webster’s Method and Divisor Geometry.

    Webster’s method is a divisor method. Unlike Hamilton’s method, which works directly with the quota vector \(q\text{,}\) Webster introduces a global scaling factor called the divisor.
    🔗
    We begin with a population vector \(p=(p_1,p_2,p_3)\) and a seat total \(M=4\text{.}\) For a chosen divisor \(d \gt 0\text{,}\) we define scaled quotas
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} q_i=\frac{p_i}{d}. \end{equation*}
    Webster then rounds each \(q_i\) to the nearest integer.
    🔗

    Definition 31. Webster Rounding Rule.

    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{W}(u)=\lfloor u+\tfrac12 \rfloor. \end{equation*}
    (When \(u\) is exactly halfway between two integers, a tie-breaking convention is required. In this activity we will avoid ties.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Step 1: Rounding Bands for One Coordinate.

    First, focus on just one coordinate. Suppose Webster outputs \(m_i=2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    29.

    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \underline{\hspace{2cm}} \;\le\; q_i \;\lt\; \underline{\hspace{2cm}}. \end{equation*}

    Step 3: From Divisor Intervals to Geometry on the Simplex.

    🔗
    \begin{equation*} m_i-\tfrac12 \;\le\; q_i \;\lt\; m_i+\tfrac12 \quad \text{for each coordinate}. \end{equation*}
